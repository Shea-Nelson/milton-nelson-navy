A family memorial

Milton Nelson

Coxswain, United States Navy

USS Noble (APA-218) · World War II

November 8, 1922 – December 23, 1997

Plank owner of an attack transport. He went to Okinawa at Easter 1945, came home to Panama City, and is buried beside his wife, Dorothy. No photograph of him has been found online. This page does not pretend otherwise.

01 — His story

A Florida sailor, and a ship that was new

Milton Nelson was born in Florida on November 8, 1922. He was nineteen when he entered the Navy, and twenty-two when his name went on the books of a ship that had just been commissioned.

Navy service, from Department of Veterans Affairs records, ran from October 6, 1942, to November 27, 1945. Where he served between October 1942 and November 1944 is not known. The paper that would say — his personnel file — has not been gathered for this page. There is a way to order it, written out below.

On August 16, 1944, he married Dorothy Tharpe in Washington County, Florida. She was Dorothy Mae (Tharpe) Nelson, born July 8, 1922. He was still in the Navy.

Inference Washington County is home country, and the wedding falls in the middle of his enlistment. The family reads it as leave. A leave paper has not been seen.

On November 27, 1944, in San Francisco, USS Noble was commissioned. The 1946 cruise book’s “Crew Muster Roll Upon Commissioning” lists “NELSON, Milton, S1c.” S1c is Seaman, First Class. A sailor on the books the day a ship is placed in commission is a plank owner. That roll is how the family knows he was one.

He later made coxswain. In the wartime Navy that was a petty officer third class, pay grade E-4 — the title then used for what was afterward called boatswain’s mate third class. The date he made the rate is not known. It is not known whether he was already a coxswain when Noble landed the 6th Marine Division at Okinawa. The bronze marker on his grave uses the rate he finished with: COX, U.S. Navy.

The family has a framed 1944 print of the ship with his name on it. A scan is not here yet. It belongs with the family photographs, when someone can send it.

02 — Voyage of the USS Noble

From a new ship to Seattle

Sixteen stops, in the order the family takes from the 1946 cruise book, read with the Navy’s Dictionary of American Naval Fighting Ships. The line on the map is a reading order, not a logged track. Where a harbor or a longitude was never written down, the stop says so.

  1. Noble was laid down July 20, 1944, as Maritime Commission hull 566, by Permanente Metals Corporation at Richmond — a Kaiser yard — and launched October 18. She was a modified Victory ship, completed by the Kaiser Shipyard there. DANFS records the ship. It does not place Milton at the yard. He is first named, so far as this page knows, on commissioning day.

  2. Acquired by the Navy and commissioned the same day, November 27, 1944. The cruise book’s crew muster roll upon commissioning, at San Francisco, lists “NELSON, Milton, S1c.” The Navy took her into service under Commander Solomon S. Isquith. Mrs. Maxine C. Jones was her sponsor. A page of that cruise book is linked, not copied: 1946 USS Noble cruise book, page 46.

  3. Shakedown off San Pedro. The summaries used here do not give the dates, only the place in the order of the cruise: after San Francisco, before the training off San Diego.

  4. Amphibious training off San Diego. This is where an attack-transport boat crew practiced the run to the beach. Whether Milton was already coxswain of one of the boats is not known.

  5. She crossed the equator on February 13, 1945. About 450 men became shellbacks that day, the Navy’s old word for a sailor who has crossed the line. The cruise book does not give the longitude, so there is no honest pin. The stop stays on the timeline.

  6. Noble embarked 1,409 men of the 6th Marine Division at Guadalcanal. The summaries do not name the beach or the day. The pin marks the island, near the old Lunga anchorage, not a precise berth.

  7. She went by way of Ulithi, the great fleet anchorage in the western Carolines. The cruise-book summary used here does not give the date she lay there.

  8. Easter Sunday, April 1, 1945. The landing went in at 8:30 in the morning, on the Hagushi beaches. Noble spent five days unloading under constant air raids and took no damage. The ship’s one World War II battle star is for Okinawa Gunto. It is not known whether Milton was already a coxswain.

  9. After Okinawa she called at Pearl Harbor, then went on to the West Coast.

  10. Leave in San Francisco in June. This is the same city as stop 2, a second call, so the pin sits a little apart from the commissioning mark.

  11. She returned to Okinawa. On August 14 and 15, 1945, the ship was under Japanese air attack. The April landing had ended with no damage. These summaries do not say whether the August attacks touched her.

  12. Occupation troops, and then the men she would take south: about 450 freed Allied prisoners of war. DANFS says she helped carry released Allied prisoners from Korea to the Philippines. The harbor is not named in the summary used here. The pin marks Korea, not a berth.

    Years later Milton told his son Kenny Nelson about a freed prisoner he was sorry for. That telling is family oral history, not a log entry. The family hears it as belonging to this voyage. That part is an inference.

  13. She carried those prisoners — about 450, including survivors of Bataan and Corregidor — to Manila. Admiral Kinkaid visited the ship there.

    Kenny Nelson’s memory of those weeks is told, carefully, under Family memories.

  14. She rescued downed Army fliers off Formosa. The exact position is not in the summary, so the pin sits offshore of the island and is marked as approximate.

  15. Noble was present for the Japanese surrender at Tsingtao in October 1945. The photograph in the gallery shows the ceremony of the 6th Marine Division. It does not show the ship, and it does not show Milton.

  16. Seattle, November 15, 1945. The cruise book notes that a large group of ship’s company was detached on points. Milton’s discharge date is November 27, 1945.

    Inference The dates sit twelve days apart. The family treats Seattle as his way home. That is a reading of two records beside each other, not a line on a discharge paper this page has seen. DANFS adds that after the war Noble took part in Operation Magic Carpet, the fleet’s work of bringing servicemen back to the United States.

Choose a stop to move the map. Stop 5, the equator, has no pin.

Inference In the fall of 1945, Noble carried about 450 freed Allied prisoners of war from Korea to Manila, among them survivors of Bataan and Corregidor. See stops 12 and 13. 04 — The work

What a coxswain did

On an attack transport, the boats were the point of the ship. Noble’s work was to arrive with troops and put them on a beach in order.

A coxswain had charge of one landing craft and the three sailors in it with him. The Navy’s wartime boat was the LCVP, the Higgins boat: one coxswain, one engineer, two deckhands. The Dictionary of American Naval Fighting Ships credits Noble with 24 landing craft, and calls that boat group her main armament — the means of delivering troops and cargo to the beach.

The coxswain is responsible for the boat. As senior member of the crew what he says goes.

— Skill in the Surf: A Landing Boat Manual, Chapter IV, “Know Your Job,” Naval History and Heritage Command

The same chapter tells the coxswain to learn the boat the way he knows a familiar meal: the daily check, the rules of the road, lowering from the transport, coming alongside, beaching, and backing off the beach. It also says the wise coxswain remembers that the crew wins by teamwork. In a real landing any one of them might have to take another’s place.

The manual wanted a coxswain who was sturdy, sharp-eyed, and able to lead. High school was preferred. Experience in small boats, or even driving a heavy truck, counted. Milton’s own path into the rate — when he was rated, and which boat was his — is not in the papers this page has.

Not known Nothing here says he steered a particular boat at Okinawa. He was a seaman first class when the ship was commissioned. He was a coxswain by the time the Navy was done with him. The months in between are the gap a personnel file could close.

07 — The ship

USS Noble (APA-218)

She was named for counties — Noble County in Indiana, in Ohio, and in Oklahoma — not for an admiral. Indiana’s county remembers Noah Noble, an early governor; Ohio’s remembers James Noble, an early settler; Oklahoma’s remembers John Noble, Secretary of the Interior from 1889 to 1893.

Noble was a Haskell-class attack transport, hull symbol APA-218, Maritime Commission type VC2-S-AP5. DANFS gives her as 455 feet 3 inches long, beam 63 feet 4 inches, draft 24 feet, speed 18 knots, complement 536. Her guns were a single 5-inch and twelve 40-millimeter. Her 24 landing craft were the armament that mattered on a beach.

The only photographs of Noble herself that could be found online were made after Milton left. The earliest is May 25, 1946, at anchor off New York, six months after his discharge. Another, off San Diego, is the later 1940s or early 1950s. She stayed in the Navy for years after he came home. Those later years are not his story, and they are not told here except to date the pictures honestly.

A sister ship, USS Sibley (APA-206), was fitting out at the same Richmond yard on September 26, 1944, two months before Noble’s commissioning. She stands in the gallery for the yard, because a wartime photograph of Noble on the ways was not found. An unidentified Haskell-class ship, photographed in 1944 or 1945 just after completion, shows the class as Milton would have known the silhouette.

One battle star, for the assault and occupation of Okinawa Gunto, April 1–5, 1945.

Likely, not confirmed The medals that usually followed that service are the Asiatic-Pacific Campaign Medal with one star, the American Campaign Medal, and the World War II Victory Medal. No award citation for Milton has been seen. Until the personnel file is read, they stay likely, not certain. The medal photographs are of the decorations, not of anything pinned on him.

08 — After the war

Panama City

He came home to Bay County. The 1950 census finds him in Panama City, age 27, head of household.

The census lists his wife as Dorothy M., and two sons: Mitchell R., age 4, and Ronald E., age 2. Milton’s occupation is hard to read. The handwriting looks like a paper-mill job and has been read as “cook, digester.” That reading is uncertain.

The family remembers three sons: Randy, Ronnie, and Kenny. Kenny’s full name is Robert Kenny Nelson. This page does not try to line the census names up with the names the family uses, beyond setting both down. Milton’s grandson Shea, and other grandchildren, keep this history. No other details of living relatives are printed here.

Dorothy died March 2, 2009. She lies beside him at Evergreen Memorial Gardens in Panama City.

09 — The file

How to request his records

The dates he made coxswain, the ships before Noble, and a real medal list, if they survive, are in his Official Military Personnel File.

Milton left the Navy on November 27, 1945. That is more than 62 years ago, so the file is archival. Anyone may order a copy — a grandchild, or a person who never met the family. The National Archives explains it on Request Military Service Records.

The public fee, from that page as reviewed in November 2026, is for the photocopy of the whole file:

  • A routine file of five pages or fewer: $25.
  • A routine file of six pages or more: $70. Most files are in this group.

Ordering the copy is not free. Seeing an archival file in person at the National Personnel Records Center, 1 Archives Drive, St. Louis, Missouri 63138, does not use that copy fee. Call the Archives’ public line, 314-801-0800, before a trip. Hours change, and the number belongs to the Archives, not to the family.

What the staff can use:

  1. Name during service: Milton Nelson.
  2. Branch: United States Navy.
  3. Dates of service: October 6, 1942 – November 27, 1945.
  4. Born November 8, 1922, in Florida.
  5. Ship, once he is on her: USS Noble (APA-218), from November 27, 1944.

A service number is not in the papers used for this site. The request can still be made. Ask, when the file comes, for the rate changes, the duty stations before Noble, and the decorations. Those three lines are the ones this page had to leave open.
